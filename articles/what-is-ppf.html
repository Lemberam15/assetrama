What is PPF? The 15-year account that pays nothing to the taxman

A government-backed account, a rate reset every quarter, and interest that is never taxed. Here is how PPF actually works — including the rules most people learn too late.

ARAsset RamaFinance education · India Published 16 Nov 20266 min read

The Public Provident Fund — PPF — is a 15-year savings account backed by the Government of India. You put in up to ₹1.5 lakh a year, it pays a fixed rate that is reviewed every quarter (7.1% for October–December 2026), and the interest and the maturity amount are never taxed. It is the closest thing India has to a guaranteed, tax-free, long-term deposit — which is why it is usually the first place salaried money goes after the emergency fund.

What PPF actually is

It is a small savings scheme run by the central government through post offices and authorised banks. Your money is not lent to a company or invested in the market; it sits with the government and earns the notified rate. You can open one at a post office or at most banks, in your own name or for a minor child.

Two features do the work. First, the rate is fixed by the government every quarter, so it does not fall because the market fell. Second, the whole thing is exempt-exempt-exempt: what you put in, what it earns, and what you take out are all outside the tax net, within the limits.

Key factPPF is not a market product. There is no NAV, no fund manager and no bad year. The trade-off is the opposite one: the rate is modest, and your money is locked for 15 years.

The rate — and why the 5th of the month matters

The rate for October–December 2026 is 7.1% a year, compounded annually, and it has not moved since April 2020. The Finance Ministry reviews small savings rates every quarter, so the figure on this page is the current one, not a promise for the next 15 years. Unlike an FD or an NSC, where the rate on the day you invest is locked for the whole term, a PPF balance earns whatever rate is notified each quarter.

Interest is credited once a year, on 31 March, and it is calculated on the lowest balance held between the 5th and the last day of each month. That single sentence is worth money: a deposit made on the 3rd starts earning from that month, while the same deposit made on the 8th earns nothing for that month. If you invest a lump sum, do it before the 5th of April, not at the end of March.

Worked example₹1,00,000 left in PPF for the full 15 years at 7.1%, with no further deposits, becomes about ₹2,79,800 — and every rupee of that interest is tax-free. The same ₹1,00,000 in a 7.1% FD would be taxed on the interest every year at your slab rate.

Notice what the lock-in really means. You are not choosing between PPF and a liquid fund — you are choosing between a 15-year commitment and everything else. If the money might be needed for a house or a wedding in five years, PPF is the wrong container, however good the tax treatment looks.

How PPF is taxed

PPF is the classic EEE product — exempt at investment, exempt at accumulation, exempt at maturity:

  • Contribution: deductible up to ₹1.5 lakh a year under Section 80C, in the old regime only. The new regime allows no 80C deduction at all, so a PPF deposit saves you nothing in tax there.
  • Interest: tax-free every year. Nothing to declare, nothing to pay.
  • Maturity: the entire balance, including 15 years of interest, is tax-free in both regimes.

That last point is why PPF still makes sense even in the new regime. A 7.1% FD is not really 7.1% — for someone in the 30% slab, the post-tax return is closer to 5%. PPF's 7.1% stays 7.1%, and because it compounds untaxed, the gap widens every year. Which regime you are actually in decides whether the 80C deduction is worth anything to you.

What ₹1.5 lakh a year becomes

Put ₹1.5 lakh in at the start of every financial year for 15 years — ₹22.5 lakh in total — and at 7.1% compounded the balance is roughly ₹40.7 lakh at maturity. None of that interest is taxable. Spread the same money as ₹12,500 a month instead and you land at about ₹40.2 lakh, a little lower because the year's money arrives later.

Who PPF suits — and who it does not

PPF suits anyone who wants a guaranteed, tax-free rupee amount at a known date: a retirement cushion, a child's education corpus, the debt half of a portfolio. It suits a salaried person in the old regime who has 80C room left and no better use for it.

It does not suit money you may need inside five years, or a young investor with a 25-year horizon who needs equity's growth and can tolerate the swings. A 15-year lock-in is a real cost, and the partial-withdrawal rules are tighter than most people assume when they open the account. An FD is more flexible; a mutual fund is more flexible still, with no guarantee at all.

Quick recap

  1. PPF is a 15-year government-backed account, one per person, ₹500–₹1.5 lakh a year.
  2. The rate is reviewed quarterly — 7.1% for October–December 2026 — and can change for your whole balance.
  3. Deposit before the 5th of the month: interest is computed on the lowest balance between the 5th and month-end.
  4. Contributions get 80C only in the old regime; interest and maturity are tax-free in both.
  5. Loans from year 3, partial withdrawal from year 7, full exit at 15 years or in 5-year extensions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the PPF interest rate now?

7.1% a year, compounded annually, for the October–December 2026 quarter. The Finance Ministry reviews small savings rates every quarter; the PPF rate has stayed at 7.1% since April 2020. The rate can change for your whole balance at each review, so it is a current rate, not a 15-year guarantee.

How much can I put into PPF each year?

A minimum of ₹500 and a maximum of ₹1.5 lakh in a financial year, across up to 12 deposits — one a month. Anything above ₹1.5 lakh earns no interest and is returned without it.

Is PPF interest taxable?

No. PPF is exempt-exempt-exempt: the contribution qualifies for deduction under Section 80C in the old regime (up to ₹1.5 lakh), and the interest and the maturity amount are tax-free in both regimes. In the new regime you get no 80C deduction, but the interest and maturity are still tax-free.

Can I withdraw from PPF before 15 years?

Partly, from the seventh year: you may withdraw up to 50% of the balance at the end of the fourth year before the year of withdrawal, or the balance at the end of the year preceding it, whichever is lower. Full closure before 15 years is allowed only after five years and only on specific grounds, such as medical treatment or education.

What happens after 15 years?

The account matures and you can withdraw the whole balance tax-free, or extend it in blocks of five years — with fresh contributions (the usual choice) or without, when the existing balance keeps earning interest.

Can I have more than one PPF account?

No. One PPF account per person. A second account is treated as irregular: it earns no interest and does not qualify for tax benefits. A parent or guardian may open one for a minor child.
